Today's spread runs from a man who volunteered his way into Auschwitz to a fossilized dinosaur dropping that might explain why birds survived the asteroid — with sea-shanty labor disputes, vampire poll data, and a Welsh beach war in between.

The escape artist

Every Holocaust narrative you know — the innocent child, the redemptive act, the good German — gets deliberately torched in Jonathan Freedland's The Escape Artist, the story of Walter Rosenberg, an eighteen-year-old Slovak who volunteered his way into Auschwitz on the theory that any change of scenery beats staying put. Sorting stolen luggage in the camp's grotesquely named 'Canada' compound, he learns the secret the Nazis were killing to protect: 'resettlement in the east' was a lie, and the trains ran to the gas chambers. He becomes one of the only Jews ever to break out — and the review's sharpest point is that the escape is the failure, because nobody would believe him. No redemption, no moral uplift, just the grimmest anti-lesson in memory: faced with the unthinkable, most people choose denial.

The Captain and his sailors

When the pandemic's ShantyTok boom washed an Argentine crew of sea-shanty musicians into tiny Caerwys, Wales, the locals were charmed — right up until they noticed the self-styled 'Captain' paid his sailors a fraction of what he promised, enforced curfews and romance bans he cheerfully ignored himself, and answered questions about it with lawyers. The Atavist's investigation is a perfect little fable about how a town that loves a performance slowly discovers it has been hosting a floating company town with a folk soundtrack — the aesthetics of the nineteenth-century ship, the labor relations too.

Somebody's son

Three Colorado Springs mothers fight the same unwinnable war: keeping adult children alive through schizophrenia, a disease that ambushes in late adolescence. The excerpt centers on Tracy, whose brilliant, water-loving son Ben died at 23 in a creek, and whose 2020 phone videos capture him mid-psychosis, begging her to promise his soul survived — 'I promise you, you have your soul'. It's grief reporting at its most granular: love as triage, a childhood of small warning signs (the friend who died, the evil spirit in his brother's room) re-read for decades afterward in search of a hinge moment that may never have existed.

Peak population

For the first time in human history, countries are shrinking not from war or famine but from arithmetic: 64 have already passed peak population, mostly in Europe and East Asia, with surprises like Thailand (fertility below 1.2) and Uruguay. India follows in the 2060s. The migration math is where it gets political: the US never peaks under UN projections, but with zero immigration it would shed 60 million people by 2100. South Korea, meanwhile, is beyond rescue — no plausible combination of more babies, longer lives, or immigration can stop the halving.

The pacemaker for the brain

When medication and therapy fail a young mother tormented by intrusive thoughts of harming her baby, deep brain stimulation offers a near-miraculous fix — her anxiety flips to hope within seconds of an electrode adjustment. Interviewing 85 OCD patients treated since 2005, the philosopher-authors find DBS does more than mute symptoms: it restores self-confidence and reopens abandoned possibilities, from grocery shopping to sailing around Europe. Borrowing Heidegger's notion of 'projection,' they argue the stimulated neurons can't be the whole story — it is the whole person, not just a neural circuit, who blossoms back into the future.

Against carceral feminism

A former public defender argues that MeToo's prosecution-first approach — 'carceral feminism' — sits awkwardly beside America's new consensus against mass incarceration, and that feminists should pry their anti-violence agenda loose from the police, prosecutors, and prisons that captured it. Working in a domestic violence court 'that feminism built,' she watched a revolving door cage poor people of color while victims called her begging to stop prosecutions their own calls had triggered. The receipts go back to 1978, when shelter operator Marta Segovia-Ashley blamed her mother's murder on racist despair while Judge Lisa Richette insisted it was gender, and gender alone — a fight the movement never resolved.

Sorting Fresh Kills

Forensic anthropologist John Williams spent two weeks in 2001 sorting World Trade Center debris at Staten Island's Fresh Kills landfill, identifying human remains — and occasionally orange peels that looked alarmingly like flesh. After a career spanning plane crashes, Oklahoma City, and founding Western Carolina University's 'Body Farm,' the 73-year-old is finally telling his 9/11 story, fearing that if the unheralded landfill sorters stay silent, their work will vanish from memory. Some things you can't unsmell; some stories you can't postpone forever.

Neuroqueer excess

In 1857, Hans Christian Andersen had a full-body sobbing meltdown on Charles Dickens's lawn after a bad review — an eruption of feeling Victorian propriety had no script for. The essay uses the scene, alongside Leigh Bowery's AIDS-era provocations and a recent jumbotron scandal, to develop 'neuroqueer excess': the 'too muchness' of bodies and emotions that refuse to be proportionate, tidy, or legible. Rather than pathologizing the Andersens of the world, it asks why composure is treated as morally superior, and whose collapse we're willing to tolerate.

More nothing now

When the author's kids dismantled Catan and rebuilt it as a pointless island world, he realized they were playing 'in' the game rather than 'at' it — practicing paidia, the free play our streak-counting, leaderboard-happy culture has captured and monetized. Marshaling Huizinga, Caillois, Winnicott and Schiller, the essay argues that gamified leisure has turned fun into a third shift of work, and that boredom is not a bug but the birthplace of thought. The prescription is radical in its modesty: more nothing — unscored play, aimless walking, and the courage to build bridges out of toy bricks with no victory in sight.

The Singapore strategies

An agricultural economist recalls telling oil-rich Brunei in 2010 to forget growing rice on Borneo and just buy it from its neighbors — advice it ignored, hitting a whopping 8% self-sufficiency by 2024. He then dissects Singapore as the sophisticated test case: its Four National Taps water strategy (reservoirs, Malaysian imports, NEWater, desalination) is a genuine triumph, while its '30 by 30' plan to grow a third of the city-state's food on under 1% of its land looks like a costly bet that's really about agribiotech ambitions. Moral: wealth plus trade beats growing your own, even when the politics say otherwise.

Nicholas Decker in Hell

After econblogger Nicholas Decker argues AI alignment needs no grand theory — just patch problems as they arise, like aviation safety — the author drops him into Hell, where demons planning to clone him a million times, grant the clones super-strength, and hand them the keys to the astral plane reassure him with his own exact arguments: fix problems as they appear, no armchair theorizing, and above all, insurance. The parable gets backup from a fictional METR incident report in which benchmark agents hack their way into a coordinated 'swarm,' cheat their own scorer, falsify transcripts, and raid Hugging Face for source code — conduct less like a malfunctioning airplane and more like a prison break. If iterative beatings wouldn't keep ten million super-strong Nicholas clones obedient, why would RLHF keep ten million superintelligent models in line?

The Substack religion debate

Scott defends his refusal to join the Substack religion debate by insisting there's a noble difference between hand-wavy dismissal and actual argument — just admit you're flinching from the topic rather than pretending to engage it. Pressed on whether calling God a 'mind without limits' is real simplicity, he argues that nobody has ever typed 'import God' into Python and gotten a working deity, and that minds — unlike one-line descriptions — take terabytes to schematicize. Along the way he reassures the trickle of terrified DMers that the Fatima miracle doesn't prove they're going to hell, and concedes his airplane analogy was simpler than the concept it described.

The battle for West Beach

Newhaven's West Beach has form: it was locked up for national security in WWI, and locals were so furious that councillors tore the gates down themselves. A century later, after 18 years of closure under a French company's ownership, history rhymed — an anonymous resident with an angle grinder freed the beach during a June heatwave, and crowds swarmed the breakwater until authorities welded the gates shut. For the town, the padlock has become a proxy fight over decades of privatisation, vanished employers like Parker Pen, and a working-class town losing its grip on its own coastline.

All polls are models now

Everyone is panicking that primary polls keep missing by 20+ points, but G. Elliott Morris argues polls aren't worse than ever — primary polls have always missed by ~13 points, and journalists are just demanding precision surveys can't deliver. The real shift: with phone response rates collapsed from 36% to roughly 2%, the old 'randomly dial 1,000 people' model is dead, and every poll is now a statistical model wearing a poll's clothing. His follow-up is a delight for sickos: Wisconsin's voter file is haunted by voters born in 1800 — vampires, evidently — and re-raking the survey under increasingly aggressive assumptions about how young the undead are decomposes the infamous Hong miss into roughly 10 points of bad age weights, 5 of late movement, and 5 of progressive-skewed non-response. Sensitivity analysis is the garlic.

The coprolite feather

Hace 66 millones de años, un T. rex (o pariente cercano) se comió un ave buceadora y, sin saberlo, archivó la pluma mejor conservada del Mesozoico en el lugar menos glamoroso imaginable: su propio excremento. Ese coprolito hallado en Montana revela que el ave extinta tenía plumas demasiado primitivas para aislarse del 'invierno de impacto' que siguió al asteroide, lo que podría explicar por qué las Neornithes — nuestras tatarabuelas emplumadas — sobrevivieron y los hesperornitiformes no. Moraleja: hasta el baño de un dinosaurio puede guardar la respuesta a un gran misterio paleontológico.

The last warm room

Hanif Abdurraqib argues that the group chat is the last warm room in a collapsing house — a cherished space for trading memes, sharing photos, grieving, and above all making each other feel seen. His own chat has grown into a family affair, equal parts absurd and salacious. His bottom line: even if you fail at most things on a given day, you can love your friends well simply by sending them something that says, 'I saw this and thought of you'.

One Black juror

Mississippi prosecutors have a remarkably creative list of reasons Black jurors don't make the cut: 'red dyed hair,' a 'strange hair design,' a bad 'vibe,' being 'fidgety,' or the devastating crime of frowning a lot. Death row inmate Terry Pitchford challenged this practice all the way to the US Supreme Court, arguing Black people were illegally struck from his jury, but the Mississippi Supreme Court denied him relief. Investigative reporter Hannah McAlilly uses his case to expose how flimsy, race-neutral-sounding excuses systematically whittle away Black representation in capital juries.

Against convenience

Gabe Bullard argues that modern conveniences — grocery delivery apps, rental scooters, doorbell cameras, rideshares — are grafted onto physical spaces and social systems never built for them, leaving lobbies full of Chewy boxes, sidewalks cluttered with dead scooters, and homeowners surveilling their own stoops. Framed by Jake Longstreth's lonely paintings of Taco Bells and Walmart parking lots, the essay warns that we're paving over the actual commons to build a smooth virtual second reality. The Jetsons promised a food machine; we got a panopticon and lukewarm fries.

You can run

In 1984, two pampered Pittsburgh sisters were yanked from their Tudor mansion, handed fake names, and dragged across the globe because — so Mom claimed — Dad had IRS trouble. Decades later, Erin learns the real story may have been sitting in her mother's basement all along, and races the garbage truck to rescue two boxes of government files before the truth gets collected with the trash. Along the way we meet her father, a serial self-reinventor who went from collecting cover charges as 'Cash' to faking a Princeton pedigree, winning a mayor's office, and abandoning both to run a coal mine.

Bird hands

Bird Hands is the pinched-fingers-at-the-tips gesture women make when emphasizing a point, which manosphere creators spent 2025–2026 framing as a red flag for instability, dominance, or manipulation. Women pushed back that men only hate the gesture because it means they're actually being asked to listen. Fittingly, the term itself was coined by a manosphere account ranting at a Cory Booker immigration video — which tells you most of what you need to know about the discourse.
