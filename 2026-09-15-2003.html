The six names on the door.

Two pieces mark twenty-five years since 9/11 from opposite ends of the road. Where it landed: in the East Village, Los Bomberos Primero — Engine 28/Ladder 11 — still runs calls around the clock, and painted on the steel plates of its front door are six names. Worth it because the memorial is built not from statistics but from small, stubborn details: a lieutenant who made stained glass and got his 20-year ring six days before the end, a chauffeur with 3,000 CDs who played the boomba at firehouse parties, a vent man who signed cards to his wife as "Clint Eastwood," a punk guitarist who picked up one overtime shift. Where it was born: the road from Greeley to Ground Zero starts in 1949, with Sayyid Qutb — the Islamist whose essays later inspired the attacks' architects — studying education methods in a Colorado college town he found spiritually hollow, in a city that had itself begun as a Fourierist utopian experiment. Worth it for the sheer strangeness of tracing an ideology to a yearbook photo. (The Greeley leg stops at a £4.95-a-month paywall; you decide if the origin story is worth the toll.)

Fifty-two minutes, fifty-nine seconds of darkness.

An eclipse-chaser league table ranks obsessives by lifetime minutes of totality — retired programmer Kryss Katsiavriades sits fifth in the UK with 52:59 from twenty total eclipses, his wife Talaat a few places behind — and the writer trains with the couple before joining a hundred strangers on a Spanish hillside for ninety seconds of the real thing. Annie Dillard sets the bar (a partial eclipse is to a total one what kissing is to marrying), and the piece earns its keep by actually delivering the untranslatable: a yodel from behind castle walls, a pregnant belly "kicking like wild" under the black sun, a shadow slicing a bullring below. Worth it because it does the thing it insists can't be done.

Wouldn't it be right to steal from a bank and give it to the poor?

That's the question the largest armed robber in Austrian history put to his detectives, and the Atavist never quite answers it. Gabriel E. — ex-WEGA, Vienna's SWAT — took nearly €3 million from a Loomis van with his friend's mistress, who was undone by an ATM camera catching a thief short enough to stand upright in the truck. His motive wasn't greed: after witnessing poverty while consulting for Gabon's ruling dynasty, he'd given away his Audi, his Rolex, and nearly his last suit, and decided the only thing left to give was other people's money. Worth it because the piece genuinely leaves open whether a saint with a handgun is a criminal or a prophet — staged, deliciously, in a country so rule-bound its subway runs on the honor system.

God would not put death in her body.

A cousin of the Vienna question, minus the gun: what happens when certainty you're doing right makes the visible world a suggestion? The Kerr siblings took three paths out of a devout Baptist childhood — Joel left Christianity, Aaron kept it, Rachel went hard enough into Pentecostalism to swap doctors for prayer. At a 2016 reunion she calmly denied a suspected miscarriage, drawing a line down a page: the bleeding on one side, the faith on the other. Worth it because it's tender where it could be lurid — a loving family facing the question its warmth can't answer, with a child's life on the line.

I'm staying alive until then.

Ron Fowler, 77, blood clots, isolation ward, gripping a nurse's phone: "Call tomorrow when you meet with Chuck. I'm staying alive until then." He and Chuck Deuschle, 83, blew the whistle on the Paducah uranium plant — the Kentucky complex that fueled America's nuclear arsenal while exposing its own workers for decades and concealing it. Now, with a private firm reviving the shuttered site, the last two of the four whistleblowers are racing to commit the story to the record before the record outlives them. Worth it as the price of being the person who says something — and it pairs with the firehouse door up top: both about people who showed up for work, and what that cost them.
