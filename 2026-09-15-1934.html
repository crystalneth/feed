The sound of social rigidity cracking.

That, the argument runs, is why Western culture long treated women's laughter as somewhere between a sin and a fatal medical event: Church fathers found it dangerously carnal (Umberto Eco's monk calls laughter a kind of daytime orgasm), etiquette manuals banned it, and 19th-century newspapers reported women literally dying of it. A laughing woman, peasant, or worker is briefly outside the established order — hence the panic, from Athenagoras calling woman mere "ground" for sowing, all the way to the scrutiny of Hillary Clinton's chuckle. Salvation came not via theory but via cinema and TV, which taught the author's never-laughing Portuguese grandmother to laugh at last, at a bad hotel joke in hospital. It earns its slot as pure forgotten-social-history: one emotion, policed for two millennia.

One human hand against 250 million songs.

Radio Paradise is William Goldsmith's 25-year bet that what plays next should remain an exercise in human judgment: a single curator using EDM-style harmonic mixing to sequence Warren Zevon into Senegalese psychedelia, under one rule — nothing should compete. The profile is stocked with the good stuff: the pioneering flower breeder's "great disappointment" of an eldest son, the transmitter he hacked together at 11 (FCC agents came round and seemed highly amused), the mid-30s music theory class that revealed "something larger at work," and a pre-Burning Man RV that's equal parts Mad Max and Marconi. Surfaced as the internet-craft slot, from the exact moment — 100,000 new tracks a day — when algorithmic curation seems inevitable.

Four words for sadness.

Arabic distinguishes past-tense grief (huzn) from present-tense suffocation (ghamm), face-visible dejection (ka'aba), and regret-laced sorrow (asaf) — distinctions that sentiment analysis, the author's own research tool, flattens into "negative," along with the theological weight of diq alsadr, the Quranic constriction of the chest as a condition of the whole inner person. The proposed fix is medieval: al-Kindi's 9th-century manual on dispelling sorrows reads like cognitive therapy, except it never divorces psychology from meaning and virtue. There's a real kinship with Goldsmith above — both pieces are about what computation can't hold — and with the laughter piece too: one tradition policed an emotion out of existence, another refined it into precision instruments. Here for the language slot, with a philosophy payoff; when someone calls you mufalsaf, take it as a compliment.

Kant thought selling your hair was wrong.

The question of whether you owe yourself anything splits the professionals: skeptics wield the "paradox of self-release" (if you owe yourself a favor, why not just waive it?), Kant counted suicide, masturbation, and even selling one's hair as violations of the duty of self-respect, and Confucius made self-cultivation a moral end. Ordinary people have already ruled: over 85% of US adults say failing to care for your health or safety is a wrong done to you, and they'd convict even a solitary Robinson Crusoe — the dividing line being harm to yourself. That cuts both ways: self-care-as-debt might get you to the gym, or make you your own harshest judge when illness gets read as moral failure. Al-Kindi would recognize the fight; he never separated the psyche from virtue in the first place. Surfaced for the philosophy slot, with actual survey data attached.
