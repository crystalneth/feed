Today, three long-form American stories about conviction — and, twice, about where grief goes when it has nowhere else to.

Past lives: $40.

Cassadaga, Florida — the self-declared Psychic Capital of the World — exists because in 1875 a Baptist-turned-medium took his spirit guide's real estate advice and led fellow Spiritualists into the Florida scrub. The charter came in 1894, and remarkably little has changed: some 200 residents keep the doctrine of continual life — death as a change of address, not an ending — on streets where Spiritualist Street crosses Mediumship Way and Harmony Hall advertises past lives at $40. The Reverend Louis Gates, 74, keeps house rules for his spirits ("if they break anything, I ask them to leave") and buries an "entertainment purposes only" disclaimer at the bottom of his gloriously blocky website. It's here because the town is a living fossil of the Fox sisters' 1848 knocking and the post-Civil War grief economy: religion offered faith in an afterlife, but Spiritualism offered the possibility of speaking to it.

Pool sharks, Boy Scouts, and aspiring saints.

When Paul Good — a pool shark so good he had to travel farther and farther to find marks — was killed outside Saigon in June 1967, months shy of twenty, his mother woke that morning with the premonition already on her. His brother Jim, a seminary washout with a chronic inability to obey orders, decided to make the government feel like it wasn't as secure as it thought it was: he recruited a crew of scrupulous Buffalo Catholics to destroy the paper files powering the draft machine, in the spirit of the Berrigan brothers' blood-and-napalm raids. Now in his eighties, he can't say how many government offices he broke into. It surfaced as the grief story's other branch — where Cassadaga's founders answered Civil War mourning with a phone line to the dead, Jim answered Vietnam mourning by going after the machine that had called up his brother — and because the piece insists, right off the bat, that this is a story about Catholics.

The taxidermist who couldn't make sausage.

In 1987 a U.S. Fish and Wildlife special agent named George Morrison opened a taxidermy and wild-game sausage shop in Fort Garland, Colorado, as "John Morgan" — and was promptly recruited for a midnight deer kill by the deputy sheriff himself, who offered to tip him off about raids and vowed that if he ever sniffed out a federal agent, he'd shoot him and leave him in a gut pile. Morrison didn't know the first thing about sausage; that's what the sidekick was for. Working a fake identity, a reformed poacher, and zero backup, he set out to take down dozens of the West's worst poachers in a valley primed by decades of land feuds to hate outsiders — having been warned that discovery meant being taken on "in numbers or from afar with a rifle in the dark of the night." It's the mirror of Jim Good's crusade, one man undercover for the government and another breaking into its offices, and it surfaced because undercover epics usually star drugs, not elk chorizo.
