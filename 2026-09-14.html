Today's brief

2026-09-14

Daniel Böttger's update on his own brain cancer is the rare first-person case study: the craniotomy that removed a fist-sized astrocytoma also took his right amygdala as collateral, and with it — apparently — his fear, his disgust, and his lifelong depression. He's now, in his words, suspiciously well, and publishing himself as a contribution to the study of low-suffering phenotypes, kin to Jo Cameron. The journey there is grotesque in the best way — bacterial skull colonies, a leaking green 3D-printed implant, skipping sedation before a 6%-mortality operation to recite a German poem — and it leads the brief because nobody else is writing their own amygdalectomy advocacy.

Archaeologists puzzled for a decade over a grooved 35,000-year-old mammoth-ivory rod from Hohle Fels until someone threaded cattail fibers through it and produced five meters of rope in ten minutes — the Stone Age's spinning jenny. The larger case: cordage was humanity's quiet revolution, from a Neanderthal's three-ply braid 50,000 years back to hafting marks 250,000 years back, and so labor-hungry it dictated when nomads could move. We only call it the Stone Age because stone is what survived. Here because "soft technology" is revisionist prehistory at its best.

The four-color theorem remains math's zombie — computer-proved amid scandal, re-proved simpler in 1997, and still infecting researchers who think a child-simple claim deserves a human-readable reason. A Danish-Japanese-Canadian team has now shipped another computer proof, messier than its predecessors ("used electricity liberally," as one computer scientist put it), but with real prizes: a coloring algorithm that drops from n² to n log n, plus fresh structural insight into planar graphs. Worth it because even the failures — Kempe's 1879 proof, believed for eleven years — are great lore.

Also new in math: OpenAI says 10,000 autonomous agents found a singularity in the Navier-Stokes equations, a Millennium Prize problem, with the proof formally checked in Lean. The announcement landed twelve hours after a rival AI-assisted team's near-miss, and both groups essentially speedran a strategy built by two humans, Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa — one of whom a rival now says deserves a Fields Medal. Where the four-color proof left mathematicians queasy about trusting machines, this one at least ships a verifier. Surfaced because AI-claimed Millennium prizes currently have a sample size of one.

Meanwhile, the machines doing this math remain unreadable to their makers. Scott Alexander's field guide to mechanistic interpretability — the science of reading an AI's mind — covers linear probes ("the dumbest possible technique"), which work as crude lie detectors until the model simply relocates its concepts, and traces the field's arc from 2023 euphoria to the 2024-25 discovery that its mapped "features" were vaguer than hoped. A neat bookend to Böttger: one mind volunteering its internals, the other refusing to be read. Worth it as a jargon decoder, plus the case study of Anthropic probing whether Claude knew it was being evaluated.

Strangest sports story of the batch: Michael Barisone, a leading dressage trainer, shot his student Lauren Kanarek twice in 2019, was found not guilty by reason of insanity despite no history of mental illness — and the dressage world largely sided with him, treating the woman he shot as the villain because she was annoying on Facebook. His stated ambition is now "the biggest comeback story in the history of sports." It's here as a masterclass in a closed community's moral physics, and for the aside that competitive dressage resembles a D.M.V. road test — with horses.

Vienna answers with the counter-heist: Austria's largest armed robbery was undone by an ATM camera catching a thief short enough to stand upright in the getaway van — a giveaway for the 5'3" former route-scheduler, who fingered her accomplice, an ex-SWAT officer who confessed he'd stolen nearly €3 million to build an orphanage in Africa. The motive sounded like a desperate lie; it was entirely true. Pair it with the dressage piece — two crime features where the least plausible explanation turns out to be the operative one. Worth it for the portrait of rule-worshipping Austria producing its own Robin Hood.

In 1928 a Brazilian army officer drove out of Rio toward Washington, DC to prove a Pan-American Highway possible; the trip took nearly a decade and 17,769 miles, with disassembled Fords loaded onto mules where roads gave out. Washington's reward was to insist he was lying. Leônidas de Oliveira died a forgotten functionary, and the Darién Gap still breaks his highway a century on — though plenty of North Americans fondly "remember" driving it. Surfaced because Aeon's essay is a lovely obituary for the ideal underneath: two continents, no passports.

The New Yorker's twenty-five-year ledger of the war on terror: about 7,000 American dead, nearly a million killed overall, trillions spent — and five times as many designated extremist groups as in 2001. The argument is structural rather than partisan: Bush declared war on an abstract noun, and, as one analyst puts it, when can you actually defeat "terrorism"? The one clean win — no second 9/11 — proves the homeland was defended, not the war won. It's here because it's a ledger, not a hot take, and a cousin to the Highway of Friendship: official optimism, measured against a reality that never signed on.

Palate cleanser. A Toronto Star reporter's mustard chicken went wrong, and a months-long investigation found his Costco jar of Maille Dijon was roughly ten times spicier than it should be — confirmed by blind test with a professional taster — with detours through one of Canada's largest mustard mills (mind the mustard dust burns) and the glucosinolate chemistry behind the heat. Unilever fields some pointed questions. It's here because it's the food world's perfect whodunit.

Back to policy, with a flaw visible from the vacant lot: FEMA's buyout program was built for traditional homeowners, so mobile homes — the country's largest stock of affordable housing — count as personal property, like boats, and park owners collect nothing for lost rent. After Hurricane Helene washed trailers off a North Carolina park, new trailers moved right back onto the same flood-prone spots. Like the war on terror, it's a program whose founding category guarantees the outcome. Surfaced because the park's owners, asked about it, declined to comment — which is the comment.

A physician's essay on hunger as a cognitive condition — not a diet phase but ambient fog, the refrigerator hum you stop hearing — runs a lifetime of starvation rewarded with applause: the poolside "new figure" at eleven, a colleague praising the slimness left by norovirus. Her point isn't that hungry women are irrational but the opposite: thinness is the one currency that reliably pays out, in hallway compliments, while bone density earns silence. Worth it for setting the Minnesota Starvation Experiment beside politics' hair-husband-hemline problem and making both click.

Two pieces on where the self ends. Karen Barad's video essay opens with classical physics's rude secret — touch never happens, atoms merely repel — and argues from quantum entanglement that if matter has no self-contained identity, touching isn't a failed collision but what matter already is. Botany makes the adjacent case: Maria Popova on Zoë Schlanger's The Light Eaters surveys plants with dialects, kin recognition and distress signals, forcing a paradigm shift that dethrones the brain as the mind's only seat. Both surfaced because the sound of old assumptions cracking is the best sound in science writing.

Astronomy's live brawl: James Webb keeps finding "little red dots" — tiny, blindingly red smudges from the early universe that shine like whole galaxies but match nothing in the catalogs. One camp says they're black hole stars, hydrogen cocoons with a hungry black hole for a heart, possibly the birth cry of every supermassive black hole; the other says they're just ordinary ravenous black holes in dusty doughnuts, seen edge-on. The field "has gotten very polarized," which is fitting. Here because a genuine scientific fight over single pixels is the best kind.

An ACX contest finalist takes a flamethrower to translator Royall Tyler's claim that "anyone can read" The Tale of Genji: Heian court culture — suffocatingly hierarchical, aesthetics-mad, drenched in weeping — is so alien that the footnotes are load-bearing. The world's oldest novel stars a hero so magnificent that everyone constantly weeps, who seduces his stepmother and kidnaps a ten-year-old to raise as "the perfect woman." Worth it because "the past is an alien civilisation" is the review's thesis and its running joke at once.

Old gods, two fates. Atlas Obscura's calendar of genuinely local Christmas traditions — Krampus swatting bystanders December 5-6, Iceland's Yule Cat eating children who lack new clothes, a Swedish straw goat whose annual arson is a spectator sport — notes the Counter-Reformation Church absorbed an Alpine demon and gave it a job. Meanwhile a suburban park outside Madrid keeps a full Olympian pantheon among its fountains, laid out as a solar system that flatly refuses to demote Pluto, IAU ruling be damned. Both made the cut because institutions can demote gods, but apparently not parks.

A bookend to Böttger: Dostoyevsky's final short story sends a suicidal nihilist to an easy-chair, through a dream-shooting, burial and a drip of grave-water on his eye, to a twin Earth whose "children of the sun" re-teach him to love existence. He wakes weeping — the meaning of life turns out to be life itself, pity included — the one feeling his nihilism couldn't delete. Worth it because where surgeons deleted Böttger's suffering with a scalpel, Dostoyevsky's dreamer finds the machinery of pity runs deeper than belief.

Sports closer: Ben Shelton — six-four, ex-quarterback, a serve the piece compares to Patrick Mahomes — outlasted Frances Tiafoe in four sets to reach the U.S. Open final, in the first all-Black American men's semifinal in tournament history. The matchup was the story: a kid raised inside a tennis family against one who slept in his custodian father's hundred-square-foot office at a tennis center; Tiafoe survived a twenty-point, fifteen-minute game, then double-faulted on match point. Shelton barely celebrated. It's here because it's the rare match report that doubles as a portrait of two American roads into the same stadium.
