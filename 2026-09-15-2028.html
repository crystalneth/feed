The whitest thing about Rome was the paint applied later.

After Garibaldi's 1861 unification left Italian nationalists with an inferiority complex, they consciously chose ancient Rome as their new mythos and promised to "make Italy great again" — then dug up the mascot for empire, with Mussolini taking it furthest: a 1934 installation of five maps on the Basilica di Massenzio, inlaid in white travertine against black marble, the final panel finished mid-campaign in Ethiopia. Britain and America ran the same con, citing Rome to justify colonizing India and defending slavery. It surfaced here for the argument underneath — genetics, Virgil and the Romans' own geographic worldview reveal an ethnically jumbled Mediterranean cosmopolis that never heard of whiteness, and the "Aryan super-race" of philosophers like Julius Evola was invented precisely because Italians, mixed for generations with neighbors from Tangier to Istanbul, couldn't be certain of their own "purity."

Africans were writing the whole time; Europe just couldn't read them.

Europeans spent centuries insisting Africans never wrote anything — while pillaging, misreading and failing to decipher the scripts sitting in front of them. Algeria alone used four writing systems; the one indigenous to Africa, Libyco-Berber, survives only as short rock engravings and still isn't fully deciphered after 400 years, and its most important piece was sold to the British Museum for five pounds, where it sits off-display. Even Pierre Bourdieu, who lived among the Kabyle for years and backed the Amazigh cultural movement, insisted they were pre-literate — a cautionary tale about intellectuals too sure they know what's right. It surfaced as the mirror of the Rome essay above: two halves of one Mediterranean myth, one whitewashing an empire, the other erasing a coast's literature. The kicker: had Hannibal finished the job in 146 BCE, we might all be debating whether the French ever developed writing.

£1,200 for a job interview that never calls back.

If you bought British strawberries this summer, there's a good chance they were picked by a Tajik — and the pipeline to your fruit bowl runs through Dushanbe offices where recruitment is officially free. Dispatch's investigation, today's one piece of shoe-leather reporting and the reason it surfaced, follows Ibrohim Halimov, who paid a Ministry-connected fixer TJS 15,000 — roughly half an average annual salary — to reach the top of the pile, sat one interview with the British labour provider Pro-Force, and never heard back; he was among the fortunate in recovering the money at all. Two dozen men told similar stories, some paying three or four years' wages for nothing — one can no longer afford his only son's wedding, another is estranged from his family over the wasted savings. All asked for anonymity, out of shame and because in authoritarian Tajikistan, accusing officials of corruption carries obvious risks.
