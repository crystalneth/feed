The knight who invented eleven genders.

Thomas Urquhart — a 17th-century Scottish royalist knighted after a skirmish with two casualties — inherited 13,000 pounds of debt and resolved to write his way out, first via a bizarre trigonometric nomenclature (proturgetick, sindiforall, cathctobasall), then via a universal language boasting eleven genders and sixty-three advantages. The reviewer plays the biography for comedy while insisting Urquhart was genuinely serious: a daydream out of control rather than a troll. Why it surfaced: a finalist in ACX's 2026 book review contest — and the excerpt's slyest joke is that the celebrated Rabelais translation never quite arrives; the life story is the hors d'oeuvre and possibly the meal.

"Happy I am living."

A self-described wimp whose parents nightly reassured him they loved him "because you're so careful" grows up haunted by men at the far edge of risk: an ex-wingsuiter who fled to flat Minnesota after a dozen friends "went in", and a German BASE jumper who cratered into a talus field and lived to cheerfully declare his happiness. Between hot springs and a halting undergraduate-French translation of Dufourmantelle's In Praise of Risk, Laidlaw works toward a philosophy of danger measured not against death but against life — asking what risk lets us gain, not just what it threatens to take. The durable gift is the lexicon of mortality euphemisms, from "going in" to "cratering," which somehow make terminal velocity sound almost tender. Why it surfaced: a Longreads excerpt from Erratica, his new book on climbing, language, and touching stone.

Scarcity as a research grant.

A Pakistani liberal-arts professor recruits four medieval Islamic polymaths — al-Biruni, Alhazen, Avicenna, al-Khwarizmi — to argue that breadth beats specialization. The biographical evidence is delightfully ironic: poverty taught al-Biruni to build his own sextants, which let him spot that al-Khujandi's well-funded sextant sagged, while Alhazen faked madness to dodge the Mad Caliph and spent his decade of house arrest revolutionizing optics. Constraints, the essay argues, can be research grants in disguise — the same shape as the wingsuiter's Minnesota, a flat place chosen precisely because there were no cliffs. And Urquhart up top is what the polymathic instinct looks like with thirteen thousand pounds of debt and no self-doubt. Why it surfaced: Aeon's case for teaching polymathy instead of fetishizing disruption.

Joy moistens the body.

Beyond the exorcisms, medieval Europeans had a surprisingly relatable line in emotional wellbeing: stress warnings for overworked monks, anxiety-ridden merchants, and lifestyle advice involving the humors, the six non-naturals, saffron-induced happiness, and onyx-based mood management. In 1395 the wool merchant Niccolaio Martini blamed a high fever on getting "very upset" about an election result — burnout with more humors and fewer therapy apps. The hinge with the polymaths: this is the same pre-modern world that staked a possessed sailor to the ground as a cure, and in which Alhazen's feigned madness was a survival strategy. Madness as diagnosis, madness as tactic. Why it surfaced: Aeon's tour of the curio cabinet of medieval mental health.

One ship, one printing press, 130,000 souls.

In 1819, seven young New England couples sailed for Hawaii with uncanny Effective Altruist logic: pick a neglected, tractable cause area with no competing religions and low overhead. Through sheer luck — Kamehameha died and the Hawaiians abolished their own kapu religion just before the ship arrived — they got royal buy-in and set about "speedrunning civilization" with schools, hymns, chimneys, and modesty dresses for "rude giantesses." The reviewer's great-great-great-grandfather Hiram Bingham I wrote the dry 1847 memoir; the reviewer, recognizing his own patterns of thinking in Hiram's words, asks what today's aspiring world-savers can learn from the last wave of people who tried. Why it surfaced: the other ACX contest finalist — a book review that's really a mirror.

A seventh bell.

Twenty-five years on, the Ground Zero ceremony adds a seventh bell-tolling — for the thousands who have since died by suicide, or from cancer and other illnesses caused by the ravages of terrorism. The essay surveys the residue of a war on terror that killed nearly a million people while holding no one accountable: a widow still receiving calls about her husband's recovered body parts, an F.B.I. whistleblower jailed for leaking, a Marine whose rage curdled into white supremacy. The rubble was cleared, but the dust and debris of anguish and fury blew straight into today's angrier America. Why it surfaced: the New Yorker's anniversary essay, drawing on new books about 9/11's unhealed scars.

No loophole for the loophole-finder.

Daysi González knows every trick in the campaign book — irresistible mailers, "uncoordinated" coordination via hard-to-find corners of campaign websites — but no loophole exists for her own situation: her Temporary Protected Status expires September 9th after twenty-five years in America. The woman whose job is electing Democrats spends her days prepping candidates while quietly planning to sell her house, her car, and everything she owns; even her famously well-connected boss Chuck Rocha is discovering that no amount of K Street phone numbers fixes this one. She's one of about a million immigrants in the widening gyre of the delegalization drive — the angrier America the 9/11 essay watched the dust blow into.
